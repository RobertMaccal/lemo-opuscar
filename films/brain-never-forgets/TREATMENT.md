# THE BRAIN NEVER FORGETS — treatment

Golden Hour Stickman · 1080×1920 · 30 fps · 47.0 s · English · no narrator

## 1. Three candidate structures

1. **One warm day, one cold sentence (chosen).** Linear: exam → bus → evening routine → bed → the brain speaks → horror → black. Every beat before 0:30 is built to make the viewer relax with him. The joke is a single false assumption ("it went well") broken by one line.
2. **Cold open on the horror face, then "earlier that day".** Hooks harder in the first second, but it tells the audience something is coming and kills the contrast that *is* the joke. Rejected.
3. **Expectation / reality split screen.** Left: what he thinks he wrote; right: the truth. Clever, but it explains the gag before it lands and needs captions to work. Rejected.

Why 1: the comedy is a contrast of feelings, and a contrast only works if the first half is fully believed. The hook in the first 3 s is not a gag but beauty: a sunlit close-up of a pen finishing a sentence, the bell, the satisfied smile.

**Opening:** extreme close on the exam booklet in a sun patch, pen finishing the last line (handwriting unreadable, shallow focus), the final dot lands on the bell.
**Ending:** the eyes snap open, a tiny punch-in, one strike, hard cut to black, silence. No resolution.
**Score shape:** Chopin's Nocturne in E-flat, Op. 9 No. 2 (public-domain composition, CC0 recording) for 0:00–0:25, fading under the bed; an original drone + sparse dissonant piano for the night, rising under the revelation, cut to nothing on "dyspnea", one strike, silence.

## 2. Logline and arc

A student is sure he aced his exam — right up until his own brain, at the edge of sleep, casually corrects him.

- Setup (0–26 s): confidence. Hand-in, "Easy, bro. Piece of cake.", teeth, water, bed.
- Turn (26–40 s): the brain, a cute pink inner voice in a violet mind-space, casually asks if he's asleep — then, terrified for him, trembles out the correct answer.
- Ending (40–44 s): a 0.7 s memory flash of the question he got wrong, the eyes snap open, strike, black.

## 3. Benchmark (grammar only)

- **Sitcom cold opens and the held reaction shot**: learn how long to hold a face before the cut; how a deadpan line lands harder over silence.
- **Short-form animated comedy on vertical platforms**: learn the economy — one location per idea, no shot longer than it needs.
- Don't take: any characters, layouts, melodies, specific shots or catchphrases.

## 6. Cue map

- **Nocturne** (Andante, ~♩=60 in 12/8 as recorded): starts at 0.0 at a gentle level, ducks 6 dB under the bus dialogue, slowly fades from 24.0 to silence at 27.0.
- **Night drone** (original, numpy): from 25.5, sine pair a minor second apart (A1/B♭1 detuned) + filtered noise, −40 dB → −30 dB by 35.6; sparse piano-like notes (synth bell tones, E♭5, D5, A4 — the tritone ghost of the Nocturne key) at 28.4, 31.9, 34.6.
- **Rising tension** 36.1–39.5: drone gains a semitone cluster and a slow 0.5 dB/s crescendo.
- **Cut** at 39.55 (just before "dyspnea"): music drops to near silence so the word lands dry.
- **Strike** at 42.45: low hit (40 Hz sine drop + body), high sting (bright noise burst + inharmonic partials), short reverb tail 2.2 s. Nothing after.

## 9. Direction change (from the user, after the first voice pass)

The brain's last line is delivered **terrified**, not deadpan: it is scared for its owner. "Hey… you sleeping?" stays casual and harmless, so the turn is now inside the brain too — it starts relaxed and panics as it says the answer. The joke stays a contrast: the first half of the film is fully safe, and the dread arrives through the brain's voice before it hits his face. No sinister laugh, no villainy: fear on his behalf.

## 10. Medical note

Hypotension = abnormally low blood pressure. Dyspnea = the sensation of difficult or uncomfortable breathing (shortness of breath). The unseen question asks for the term for shortness of breath; he wrote "hypotension". The question is only legible in the 0.7 s flash after the brain has said the answer.
